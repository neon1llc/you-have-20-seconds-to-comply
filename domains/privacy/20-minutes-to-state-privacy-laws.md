**Read this if** your business keeps personal information about customers, clients, or employees. That's nearly every business. It matters most if you have customers in many states, run a website or app, sell online, or handle IDs, account numbers, or health details.

## There's no single U.S. privacy law

The United States has no one federal privacy law for businesses in general. Instead, a business answers to:

- **sector laws** that apply to certain kinds of data, such as HIPAA for health care, the FTC Safeguards Rule for financial services, FERPA for schools, and COPPA for children online (each has its own walkthrough in this repository),
- **Section 5 of the FTC Act**, which bans unfair or deceptive practices, including breaking your own privacy promises or failing to protect data reasonably,
- **state breach notification laws**, which every state has,
- **state data security laws**, such as New York's SHIELD Act, that require reasonable safeguards,
- and **comprehensive state privacy laws**, which about two dozen states have passed, giving residents rights over their data.

State laws follow the person, not the business. A New York company with customers in California, Texas, and Virginia may owe duties under all four states' laws.

## Breach notification: every state

All 50 states, the District of Columbia, and U.S. territories require businesses to notify people when certain personal information is breached. The details vary by state:

- **What counts.** Usually a name plus a Social Security number, driver's license or state ID number, or a financial account or card number with its access code. Many states add biometrics, medical and health insurance information, and online account credentials.
- **Encryption.** Most states don't require notice when the data was encrypted and the key wasn't taken.
- **Deadlines.** Some states say "without unreasonable delay." A growing number set a fixed limit, often 30 to 60 days.
- **Who else to tell.** Many require notice to the state attorney general, and to credit reporting agencies above a set number of residents.
- **Vendors.** A service provider that holds data for someone else usually must tell the data owner, which then notifies people.

Plan for the strictest state where you have customers, and get legal help when a breach happens.

## New York: the SHIELD Act

New York's Stop Hacks and Improve Electronic Data Security (SHIELD) Act applies to any person or business that owns or licenses computerized private information of New York residents, wherever the business is located. It has two parts.

**Breach notice (GBL § 899-aa).** "Private information" includes a name or identifier with an unencrypted Social Security number, driver's license or ID number, account or card number (with or without a code, if it can be used without one), biometric data, medical information, or health insurance information. A username or email address with its password or security answer counts on its own. Notice goes to affected residents in the most expedient time possible, and **no later than 30 days after discovery**. The business also notifies the Attorney General, the Department of State, and the State Police, plus the Department of Financial Services if it's a DFS-regulated entity, and the credit reporting agencies if more than 5,000 New Yorkers are notified at once. A business that notifies people under HIPAA, GLBA, or the DFS cybersecurity rule doesn't send a second notice to individuals, but still notifies the state agencies. HIPAA covered entities must notify the Attorney General within five business days of notifying HHS.

**Reasonable safeguards (GBL § 899-bb).** A business must keep reasonable administrative, technical, and physical safeguards for private information. The law lists examples, including designating one or more employees to run the security program, identifying risks, **training and managing employees in the security program's practices and procedures**, choosing capable service providers and requiring safeguards by contract, protecting systems and data, and disposing of private information within a reasonable time after it's no longer needed.

A **small business**, meaning fewer than 50 employees, less than $3 million in gross annual revenue in each of the last three fiscal years, or less than $5 million in year-end total assets, complies if its safeguards are reasonable for its size and complexity, the nature of its activities, and the sensitivity of the information. Businesses already complying with HIPAA, GLBA, or the DFS cybersecurity rule are treated as compliant with the safeguards section.

The Attorney General enforces both parts. For notice failures, a court can award the greater of $5,000 or up to $20 per failed notice, capped at $250,000, for knowing or reckless violations. For safeguard failures, the Attorney General can seek civil penalties under the General Business Law.

## Comprehensive state privacy laws

California's Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA), as amended by the CPRA, came first. By late 2026, about two dozen states had passed comprehensive privacy laws, including Colorado, Connecticut, Virginia, Texas, Oregon, and New Jersey. New York had not. These laws differ in detail, but they share a pattern.

**Who's covered.** Most apply only to businesses above a size threshold, commonly those that process data on 100,000 or more state residents a year, or a smaller number if they make money from selling data. California also covers businesses with annual gross revenue above $26,625,000 (the 2025 adjusted figure). Texas and a few others cover most businesses that aren't small businesses as federally defined, with some duties, such as consent for selling sensitive data, applying even to small ones. Many exempt data already covered by HIPAA, GLBA, or FERPA.

**Consumer rights.** Residents can ask to know and access the data a business holds about them, correct it, delete it, and get a portable copy. They can opt out of the sale of their data, targeted advertising, and certain profiling. Many states require opt-in consent before processing sensitive data such as health, precise location, race or ethnicity, sexual orientation, immigration status, biometrics, and data about children. Several states, including California and Colorado, require honoring browser opt-out signals such as Global Privacy Control.

**Business duties.** A clear privacy notice, collecting only what's reasonably necessary, reasonable security, contracts with processors (vendors) that limit how they use the data, and written data protection assessments for higher-risk processing such as targeted advertising, selling data, or processing sensitive data.

California's updated regulations, effective January 1, 2026, add requirements for risk assessments, cybersecurity audits, and automated decision-making technology, with deadlines phased in over several years.

**Training.** The California regulations require that everyone responsible for handling consumer privacy requests or the business's compliance knows the law's requirements and how to direct consumers to exercise their rights.

**Enforcement.** State attorneys general enforce these laws, and California also has a dedicated agency, the California Privacy Protection Agency. California's administrative fines run up to $2,663 per violation and $7,988 per intentional violation or violation involving minors. California also lets consumers sue over certain data breaches caused by unreasonable security.

## What every staff member needs to know

1. **Collect only what you need**, and don't keep it longer than needed.
2. **Know where personal data lives**, and keep it in approved systems, not personal email, desktops, or unapproved apps, including AI tools.
3. **Use strong passwords and multi-factor authentication**, and lock devices.
4. **Spot phishing.** Stolen credentials start most breaches.
5. **Share personal data only with approved vendors** that have a contract.
6. **Route privacy requests** ("delete my data," "what do you have on me," "stop selling my data") to the designated person the same day. Deadlines run from when anyone receives them, commonly 45 days to respond.
7. **Don't promise what the privacy notice doesn't say.** Breaking a privacy promise is a deception case for the FTC.
8. **Report lost devices, misdirected emails, and suspected breaches right away.** New York's 30-day clock starts at discovery.
9. **Dispose of data properly**: shred paper and wipe devices.

## What the business needs on paper

- A data inventory: what personal data you hold, where, why, and for how long.
- A privacy notice that matches what you actually do.
- A written security program, sized to the business (the SHIELD Act's list is a good outline).
- Training records. See the [training log](../../templates/training-log.csv).
- Vendor contracts with security and data use terms.
- A process and log for privacy requests, if a comprehensive state law applies.
- Data protection assessments for higher-risk processing, where required.
- An incident response plan with state-by-state notice steps.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking privacy laws only apply to big tech. Breach and safeguard laws apply to businesses of every size.
- A privacy notice copied from another website that doesn't match your practices.
- Ad and analytics tags on the website that amount to "selling" or "sharing" data under state law, with no opt-out.
- Ignoring Global Privacy Control signals where required.
- Keeping former customers' data indefinitely.
- No plan for the 30-day New York deadline.
