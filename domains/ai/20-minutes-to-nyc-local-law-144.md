**Read this if** you hire or promote people for jobs in New York City, or you're a recruiter or staffing agency placing candidates there, and you use software that scores, ranks, screens, or recommends candidates. Resume screeners, video interview scoring, and assessment tools built into applicant tracking systems can all count.

## What the law covers

Local Law 144 regulates **automated employment decision tools (AEDTs)**: computational processes based on machine learning, statistical modeling, data analytics, or AI that issue a simplified output, such as a score, classification, or recommendation, used to substantially assist or replace discretionary decision making for employment decisions. The Department of Consumer and Worker Protection (DCWP) has enforced it since July 5, 2023.

Under DCWP's rules, a tool "substantially assists or replaces" a decision when its output is the only criterion, carries more weight than any other criterion, or is used to overrule a human's conclusion. A tool that only helps a recruiter search a database, with people making the actual judgment, may fall outside the definition. Many employers apply the law's practices anyway, because the line is narrow and other laws apply.

## What it requires

1. **A bias audit within the past year.** Before using an AEDT, an independent auditor must have done a bias audit of it within one year. The audit calculates selection or scoring rates and impact ratios by sex, by race and ethnicity, and by their intersections.
2. **A public summary.** The employer posts a summary of the most recent audit results, the date of the audit, the distribution date of the tool, and the source of the data on its website's employment section.
3. **Candidate notices.** Candidates and employees who live in New York City get notice at least 10 business days before the tool is used that an AEDT will be used, and what job qualifications and characteristics it will assess. The notice must explain how to request an alternative selection process or a reasonable accommodation, if available.
4. **Data information on request.** If not posted, the employer gives information about the type of data collected, its source, and the retention policy within 30 days of a written request.

The law doesn't ban using a tool that shows disparities. Federal, state, and city anti-discrimination laws still apply, and a tool with disparate impact can create liability under them.

## Penalties

Civil penalties are up to $500 for a first violation and each additional violation the same day, and $500 to $1,500 for each later violation. Each day a noncompliant tool is used, and each failure to give a required notice, counts as a separate violation.

## What hiring teams need to know

1. **Know which tools in your hiring process score or rank people**, including features inside the applicant tracking system.
2. **Check the bias audit date** before using a tool, and that the summary is posted.
3. **Send notices 10 business days ahead**, for example in the job posting or the careers page, as the rules allow.
4. **Offer and route requests** for alternative processes or accommodations.
5. **Don't let a score decide alone** without understanding how it's used.
6. **Report concerns** about a tool treating groups differently.

## What the business needs on paper

- An inventory of hiring tools and whether each is an AEDT.
- The current bias audit for each AEDT and the posted summary.
- Copies of candidate notices and where they appeared.
- Vendor contracts that require the vendor to support audits and share data.
- Training records for recruiters and hiring managers. See the [training log](../../templates/training-log.csv).

1. How recent must the bias audit be?
2. How far in advance must candidates be notified?
3. Does the law ban using a tool whose audit shows disparities?
4. Who enforces the law?
5. Does it apply to candidates who live outside New York City?
