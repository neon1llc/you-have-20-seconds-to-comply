**Read this if** you run or work at a medical, dental, behavioral health, or therapy practice, a pharmacy, a lab, a health plan, or a billing company, or you sell any service to one of them that touches patient information. That last group includes IT support firms, cloud hosts, answering services, shredding companies, and software vendors.

## Who HIPAA covers

HIPAA's privacy and security rules apply to two kinds of organizations.

**Covered entities** are health plans, health care clearinghouses, and health care providers who send health information electronically in connection with standard transactions, such as billing an insurer or checking eligibility. A therapist who only takes cash and never bills insurance electronically may not be a covered entity. Almost every practice that takes insurance is.

**Business associates** are people and companies that do work for a covered entity and create, receive, keep, or send protected health information while doing it. A managed IT provider with access to the practice's server is a business associate. So is a cloud backup service, a transcription service, or a consultant who reviews charts. Their own subcontractors who touch the same information are business associates too.

A business associate needs a signed business associate agreement (BAA) with each covered entity it serves, and with each subcontractor it passes the information to. The BAA is a contract, and HHS publishes sample language for it.

CMS, part of HHS, has a [covered entity decision tool](https://www.cms.gov/priorities/key-initiatives/burden-reduction/administrative-simplification/hipaa/covered-entities) for the first question. If the answer is unclear, ask a health care attorney before you assume you're out.

## What counts as protected health information

Protected health information (PHI) is health information that identifies a person, or could reasonably be used to identify them, and that a covered entity or business associate holds or sends in any form: paper, electronic, or spoken.

"Health information" is broad. It covers a diagnosis, a test result, or a prescription, and also the fact that someone is a patient at all, an appointment time, a billing record, and a photo taken in an exam room. The identifiers that make it PHI include names, addresses, dates tied to the person (birth, admission, discharge), phone numbers, email addresses, account and record numbers, device serial numbers, IP addresses, and full face photos.

Two things are not PHI under HIPAA: employment records a covered entity keeps as an employer (an employee's sick note in an HR file, for example), and education records covered by FERPA.

When information has been properly de-identified under the HIPAA standard, it is no longer PHI.

## The three rules in plain words

### The Privacy Rule

The Privacy Rule says when PHI may be used and shared, and gives patients rights over their own information.

- **Treatment, payment, and health care operations.** A practice can use and share PHI for these without the patient's written authorization. Sending records to a specialist, billing an insurer, and running quality reviews all fit.
- **Minimum necessary.** For most uses other than treatment, people should see and share only the PHI the task needs. The billing clerk doesn't need the full chart. The front desk doesn't need lab results.
- **Authorization.** Marketing, most sales of PHI, and sharing psychotherapy notes need the patient's written authorization.
- **Notice of Privacy Practices.** Covered providers give patients a notice explaining how their information is used and what their rights are, and post it in the office and on the website.
- **Right of access.** Patients can see and get a copy of their records, including electronic copies in the form they ask for if the practice can produce it. The practice has 30 days to respond, with one 30-day extension if it tells the patient why in writing. The fee, if any, has to be reasonable and cost based. HHS has settled dozens of cases with practices that were slow or charged too much.
- **Other rights.** Patients can ask to correct their records, ask for a list of certain disclosures, ask for limits on sharing, and ask to be contacted a certain way (for example, only on their cell phone).

### The Security Rule

The Security Rule applies to electronic PHI (ePHI). It requires administrative, physical, and technical safeguards that fit the size and risk of the organization.

- **Risk analysis.** An accurate and thorough look at where ePHI lives and what could go wrong with it, kept current. A missing or stale risk analysis appears in a large share of HHS enforcement actions. The free [HHS Security Risk Assessment Tool](https://www.healthit.gov/topic/privacy-security-and-hipaa/security-risk-assessment-tool) is built for small practices.
- **Access control.** A unique user ID for each person, automatic logoff, and access limited to what each role needs.
- **Audit controls.** Systems that record who looked at or changed ePHI, and someone who reviews those records.
- **Integrity and transmission security.** Protecting ePHI from improper change, and encrypting it in transit where that's reasonable.
- **Contingency planning.** Backups, a disaster recovery plan, and a way to keep caring for patients when systems are down.
- **Security awareness and training** for the whole workforce, managers included.

Some parts of the Security Rule are marked "addressable." Addressable does not mean optional. It means the organization decides whether the safeguard is reasonable for it, and if not, documents why and what it does instead.

### The Breach Notification Rule

A breach is an impermissible use or disclosure of unsecured PHI. The rule presumes any such incident is a breach unless a documented risk assessment shows a low probability that the information was compromised. That assessment looks at four things: what information was involved, who received it, whether it was actually viewed or acquired, and how well the risk was reduced afterward.

If it's a breach:

- **Patients** get written notice without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 calendar days after the breach is discovered.
- **HHS** gets notice at the same time as patients if 500 or more people are affected. Smaller breaches go in a log and are reported to HHS within 60 days after the end of the calendar year.
- **The media** gets notice when more than 500 residents of one state or jurisdiction are affected.
- **Business associates** tell the covered entity, also within 60 days of discovery. Many BAAs set a shorter deadline, often 5 to 10 days.

PHI that was properly encrypted, with the key kept safe, is "secured," and losing it is not a reportable breach. That single fact makes full-disk encryption on laptops and phones one of the best investments a small practice can make.

State breach laws can add their own deadlines and notice rules on top of HIPAA.

## What every workforce member needs to know

HIPAA requires covered entities to train all workforce members on the privacy policies and procedures they need for their jobs, and to run a security awareness program for everyone. "Workforce" includes employees, volunteers, trainees, and temps, paid or not.

The rule sets three timing points for privacy training: new workforce members within a reasonable time after they start, affected staff within a reasonable time after a material policy change, and everyone when the program begins. It does not say "annually." Annual training is still the norm and the safer practice, because people forget, the threats change, and an annual record is easy to show an investigator.

These are the points a training session should leave people able to act on.

1. **Look only at what your job needs.** Opening a neighbor's, a coworker's, a family member's, or a celebrity's chart out of curiosity is a violation even if you tell no one. Record systems log every view, and these cases get found.
2. **Verify before you share.** Confirm a caller's identity before giving out information. Check the fax number, the email address, and the name on the envelope before you send.
3. **Use the approved tools.** Don't text PHI from a personal phone, email it from a personal account, or paste it into an unapproved app, including AI chat tools, unless the practice has a BAA with that vendor and approves it.
4. **Keep it out of sight and earshot.** Lock screens when you step away. Turn papers face down. Keep voices down at the front desk and in hallways.
5. **Don't post.** No photos, stories, or "guess who came in today" on social media, even without names. Details can identify a person.
6. **Dispose of it properly.** Shred paper. Hand old devices to IT for wiping.
7. **Spot phishing and report it.** Most health care breaches start with a stolen password or a malicious attachment. Report a suspicious email instead of deleting it.
8. **Report mistakes right away.** A misdirected fax or a lost phone is far easier to handle on day 1 than on day 59. The 60-day clock starts when anyone in the workforce knows, not when the privacy officer hears about it.
9. **Know your privacy officer and security officer by name**, and how to reach them.
10. **Know that patients have rights**, and send access requests to the right person promptly so the 30-day deadline isn't missed.

## What the business needs on paper

An investigator or auditor will ask to see documents. HIPAA requires you to keep required policies, procedures, and records of required actions (including training) for six years from when they were created or last in effect, whichever is later.

- A named privacy officer and a named security officer (one person can hold both in a small practice).
- Written privacy and security policies and procedures.
- A current risk analysis and a risk management plan that tracks the fixes.
- A Notice of Privacy Practices, posted and handed out.
- Signed BAAs with every business associate, and a list of who they are.
- Training records: who, what, and when. The [training log template](../../templates/training-log.csv) works for this.
- A sanctions policy for workforce members who break the rules, applied consistently.
- An incident and breach log, including the risk assessments for incidents you decided were not breaches.
- Backup, disaster recovery, and emergency operations plans.

## Penalties and enforcement

The HHS Office for Civil Rights (OCR) enforces HIPAA. Most cases start with a patient complaint or a breach report. Most end with corrective action, and some end with a settlement or a civil money penalty.

Civil penalties come in four tiers based on how culpable the organization was. After the January 2026 inflation adjustment, the per-violation amounts run from $145 at the lowest tier up to $2,190,294 for willful neglect that isn't corrected, with a calendar-year cap of $2,190,294 for violations of the same requirement. Since 2019, OCR has applied lower annual caps to the first three tiers as a matter of enforcement discretion.

Criminal penalties apply to knowingly obtaining or disclosing PHI in violation of the law. The Department of Justice prosecutes them. They reach up to $50,000 and one year in prison, up to $100,000 and five years under false pretenses, and up to $250,000 and ten years with intent to sell or use the information for gain or harm.

State attorneys general can also bring HIPAA cases on behalf of their residents.

## What's changing

- **Security Rule update.** HHS proposed a major rewrite of the Security Rule in January 2025. It would remove the addressable label from most safeguards, require encryption and multi-factor authentication with few exceptions, and require a written asset inventory and network map. As of this guide's review date it is still a proposal, and HHS's regulatory agenda has moved its target date more than once. Most of what it proposes is good practice already, so working toward it now isn't wasted.
- **Substance use disorder records (42 CFR Part 2).** A 2024 rule brought Part 2 closer to HIPAA. Programs covered by Part 2 had to comply by February 16, 2026.
- **Reproductive health privacy rule.** A 2024 HIPAA rule adding protections for reproductive health information was vacated by a federal court in Texas in June 2025. Check with counsel before changing your Notice of Privacy Practices or forms based on it.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the risk analysis as a one-time project. It needs a refresh when systems or the practice change, and at least on a regular schedule.
- No BAA with the IT provider, the cloud host, or the email service.
- Shared logins at the front desk or nurses' station.
- Unencrypted laptops, phones, or USB drives.
- Training once at hire and never again, or training with no record.
- Ignoring access requests, or charging per-page fees for electronic copies.
- Website tracking tools, such as ad pixels on appointment pages, that send visitor information to advertising companies. OCR has warned about this, and a federal court narrowed that guidance in 2024, but class action lawsuits continue.

## Check yourself

1. A receptionist looks up a friend's appointment history but tells no one. Is that a HIPAA violation?
2. A laptop holding patient records is stolen from a car. Its disk was encrypted, and the password was not with it. Is it a reportable breach?
3. How long does a practice have to respond to a patient's request for their records?
4. Your IT company can log in to the server that holds patient records. What do you need from them?
5. Does HIPAA say training must happen every year?
