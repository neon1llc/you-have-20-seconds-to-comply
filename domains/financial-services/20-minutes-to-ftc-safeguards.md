**Read this if** your business is a tax preparer or accountant, mortgage broker or lender, auto dealer that finances or leases, payday or installment lender, check casher, money transmitter, real estate appraiser or settlement company, debt collector, credit counselor, or non-bank finance company of any kind. Colleges that take part in federal student aid programs are covered too. The rule also matters to the IT providers, software vendors, and bookkeepers who work for these businesses, because the rule requires their clients to oversee them.

## What the rule is

The Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA) requires financial institutions to protect their customers' personal information. Banks and credit unions answer to their own regulators. For most other businesses that offer financial products or services to consumers, the Federal Trade Commission enforces GLBA's security requirements through the **Safeguards Rule**, 16 CFR Part 314.

The FTC rewrote the rule in 2021 to spell out specific requirements, most of which took effect June 9, 2023. A 2023 amendment added a duty to report certain security events to the FTC, effective May 13, 2024.

"Financial institution" is broader than most owners expect. The rule's own examples include tax preparation services, mortgage brokers, real estate appraisers, check cashers, businesses that wire money for consumers, retailers that issue their own credit cards, and auto dealers that lease vehicles for more than 90 days. If you're not sure whether you're covered, the FTC's business guide and an attorney can help you decide.

## What information it protects

The rule protects **customer information**: any record with nonpublic personal information about your customers, on paper or electronic, that you or your service providers handle. That includes names tied to Social Security numbers, account and loan numbers, income and tax figures, credit reports, driver's license images, and the simple fact that someone is your customer.

## The nine elements

The rule requires a written information security program that's appropriate for your size and complexity, the nature of your work, and how sensitive the information is. The program must include these elements.

1. **A Qualified Individual.** One person who oversees and enforces the program. It can be an employee, or someone at an affiliate or service provider. If you use a service provider, you still own compliance, you designate a senior person to direct and oversee them, and you require the provider to keep a program that protects you.
2. **A written risk assessment** that identifies reasonably foreseeable risks to customer information, judges how well current safeguards control them, and says how each risk will be reduced or accepted. It gets repeated periodically.
3. **Safeguards** to control the risks you found, including:
   - access controls, so people can see only the customer information their job needs,
   - an inventory of the data, people, devices, systems, and facilities you rely on,
   - encryption of customer information in transit over external networks and at rest (or compensating controls the Qualified Individual approves in writing),
   - secure development practices for software you build, and a way to evaluate software you buy,
   - multi-factor authentication for anyone accessing any information system, unless the Qualified Individual approves an equivalent control in writing,
   - secure disposal of customer information no later than two years after you last used it for that customer, unless you have a legitimate business or legal reason to keep it,
   - change management,
   - and monitoring and logging of what authorized users do, to catch misuse.
4. **Regular testing.** Either continuous monitoring, or an annual penetration test plus vulnerability scans at least every six months and after material changes.
5. **Trained staff.** Security awareness training for everyone, updated as your risk assessment finds new risks, and qualified security staff (yours, an affiliate's, or a provider's) who keep their skills current.
6. **Service provider oversight.** Choose providers that can protect the information, require safeguards in the contract, and reassess them periodically based on risk.
7. **Keep the program current** as testing, risk assessments, and changes in your business show what needs to change.
8. **A written incident response plan** covering goals, internal processes, roles and decision authority, communications, fixing weaknesses, documentation, and a review after each event.
9. **A written report to the board**, or to a senior officer if there's no board, at least once a year, on the program's status and material matters such as risk decisions, test results, and security events.

**Fewer than 5,000 consumers?** If you keep customer information on fewer than 5,000 consumers, you don't have to do the written risk assessment, the penetration tests and scans, the written incident response plan, or the annual board report. Everything else still applies, including MFA, encryption, training, and service provider oversight. Most small tax practices count consumers well past 5,000 once former clients are included, so count carefully before relying on this.

## Reporting to the FTC

Since May 13, 2024, you must notify the FTC within 30 days of discovering a **notification event** that involves the information of at least 500 consumers. A notification event is unauthorized acquisition of unencrypted customer information. Encrypted data counts as unencrypted if the key was also taken. Unauthorized access is presumed to be acquisition unless you have reliable evidence it wasn't.

The report goes through a form on the FTC's website and includes what kinds of information were involved, how many people were affected, and a general description. The FTC can make these reports public.

The clock starts when any employee, officer, or agent knows about the event, other than the person who caused it. So staff need to report problems up the chain right away.

State breach notification laws apply separately and may require notices to the affected people and to state officials.

## Tax preparers: the IRS angle

Tax and accounting firms face the same rule from a second direction. The IRS reminds preparers that the Safeguards Rule requires a written information security plan (WISP), and paid preparers agree to protect taxpayer data when they renew their Preparer Tax Identification Number each year. The IRS and its Security Summit partners publish a sample WISP for small practices (Publication 5708) and a security guide (Publication 4557). Those are the best starting point for a small firm.

## What every staff member needs to know

The rule says to train personnel in security awareness, updated as risks change. It doesn't set a frequency, so most firms train at hire and at least once a year, plus short reminders during tax season or other peak times. A session should leave people able to act on these points.

1. **Use MFA everywhere it's offered**, and never approve a sign-in prompt you didn't start.
2. **Spot phishing and pretexting.** Tax season brings fake "new client" emails with malicious attachments, fake IRS or e-file provider messages, and calls asking for client details. Report them.
3. **Verify payment and banking changes by phone**, using a number you already have, before changing where a refund, loan payout, or payment goes.
4. **Send customer information only through approved, encrypted channels**, such as the client portal. Not plain email, not personal accounts, and not unapproved AI tools.
5. **Look only at the records your work needs.**
6. **Lock screens, lock offices, and lock file cabinets.**
7. **Shred paper and hand old devices to IT** for wiping.
8. **Use only approved devices** for customer information. Personal phones and home computers need approval and protection first.
9. **Report anything unusual right away**: a lost laptop, a misdirected email, a client saying someone filed a return in their name, or a computer acting strangely.
10. **Know who the Qualified Individual is** and how to reach them after hours.

## What the business needs on paper

- The written information security program, naming the Qualified Individual.
- The written risk assessment, and records of each repeat.
- A data and system inventory.
- Written approvals for any exceptions to encryption or MFA.
- Penetration test and vulnerability scan reports, or a description of continuous monitoring.
- Training records. See the [training log](../../templates/training-log.csv) and [acknowledgment form](../../templates/acknowledgment-form.md).
- Service provider contracts with security terms, and records of periodic reviews.
- The data retention and disposal policy, and records that disposal happens.
- The incident response plan and incident records, including any FTC reports.
- Each annual report to the board or senior officer.

## Penalties and enforcement

The FTC enforces the rule through investigations and lawsuits. Most cases end with a consent order that requires a security program, independent assessments for years (often 20), and reporting to the FTC. Violating an order can bring civil penalties for each violation. The FTC has brought Safeguards Rule cases against tax preparers, auto dealers, mortgage companies, and others, and announces them publicly. State attorneys general can act under state law too, and a breach brings its own costs in notification, credit monitoring, lost clients, and lawsuits.

## Common mistakes

- Not realizing the business is a "financial institution."
- A WISP downloaded and filed, but never put into practice.
- MFA on email but not on tax software, the client portal, or remote access.
- Former clients' files kept forever, on paper and on old computers.
- An IT provider with full access and no contract terms about security.
- Counting only current clients when deciding on the under-5,000 exemption.
- No plan for the 30-day FTC deadline.
